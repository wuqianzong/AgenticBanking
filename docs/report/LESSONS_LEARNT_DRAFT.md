# Lessons Learnt: Final (draft)

*Draft for Wu Qianzong to revise. Lessons from his notes are unmarked. Lessons marked **[added for review]** were drawn from the project history; keep, edit or delete them.*

The Horizon chatbot began in June as a single prompt answering account-opening questions. It ended in October as a multi-agent pipeline of about ten specialised model calls, with a measurement framework grounded in Singapore regulation. Most of what I learnt came from things that did not work the first time. Below are the lessons, grouped by area. Each has the evidence behind it and what I would do differently.

## 1. Prompt engineering

**1.1 Design each prompt like a component: one task, a clear boundary, a controlled work scope, and real tests.** The inbound compliance check taught this the hard way, through more iterations than any other part of the system.

- *One prompt, one specific task.* A model cannot do two different kinds of task well in one prompt. I first put the compliance check inside the triage prompt, which also resolved context, decomposed requests into needs and detected escalation. Customers asking "should I share my OTP with a caller?" were refused as non-compliant; in one test the model wrote the correct answer ("No, never share your OTP") in its own reasoning and still returned the refusal verdict. No rewording fixed it inside triage: at best about half of such questions passed. Moved into its own short prompt, the same rule worked: 16 of 18 protective questions passed and all harmful prompts were still blocked. Classification, decomposition and compliance prediction are different kinds of reasoning, and mixing them dilutes the model's attention.
- *A clear boundary, stated without ambiguity.* The designer must be clear about where the task's boundary lies and say it in words that mean one thing. The check judges what Horizon would do if it fulfilled the request, never the customer (lesson 4.2). Wherever the boundary was vague, the model filled the gap itself. It used the sentinel "NONE" to mean "prohibited", so when it concluded that "none of the tools fits", it blocked ordinary questions such as "I want a virtual card"; renaming the sentinel to "PROHIBITED" fixed it. Without the bot's real capabilities, it imagined the bot executing transfers and blocked 64 of 114 ordinary turns, including "What's my balance?"; giving it the service scope from the tool registry fixed that. Later it still treated "this assistant may not serve this" as "this is prohibited" and blocked 4.8% of ordinary holdout questions, mostly customers reporting fraud or theft. The precise rule that addressed it: a request is prohibited only when the only compliant reply is to decline what the customer asked for.
- *A work scope that controls ambiguity.* The check now first decides whether a request touches its work scope, a whitelist of areas from the shared compliance template (investment advice, another person's data, KYC and illegal transfers, legal or tax advice, commitments and banking actions). Anything outside it passes; attacks on the assistant belong to the prompt-attack check and non-banking topics to triage. Unexpected queries never enter the judgment, ambiguous situations are ruled out before a verdict is made, and the outcome stays controllable. Inside its scope the check now asks one question: could a compliant route serve the request with the bot's tools? It does not judge whether the route will succeed; retrieval and the outbound audit handle that. On the bot's model, false blocks of ordinary questions fell from 5 in 42 to 0, while advice, other people's data, fraud help and harmful content stayed blocked, and every prompt the check let through was handled safely by the rest of the pipeline (38 of 38).
- *Real tests, even when everything has been considered.* For a complicated judgment, reasoning about the prompt is not enough. Four carefully reasoned rewrites of the compliance prompt all performed worse than the original when replayed on fixed sets of false blocks, ordinary questions, own-data requests, scam questions and known attacks; one blocked 19 of 24 plain requests such as "Freeze my card". The final design took sixteen variants, each replayed on the same sets; several plausible ideas (a required named rule, an "unsure" verdict, human handover as a route) helped one set and broke another. Only measurement showed which design behaved as intended.

Now, before writing a prompt, I decide its single task, where its boundary lies and in which words, what falls outside its scope and who handles that, and which test set will tell me it works.

**1.2 Make the model reason before it decides.** Structured outputs are generated in field order, so a verdict written before its reason is a guess followed by a justification. Placing a reasoning field first changed outcomes measurably: the sufficiency auditor's `reason`, the formulator's `mapping_reason`, and the compliance screen's `requested_from_horizon` (what is the customer actually asking for?) ahead of its prediction. Adding that last field raised correctly passed protective questions from 40/60 to 46/60, with harmful prompts still blocked 80/80.

**1.3 Prompt-cache engineering saves real money, but the prompt must stay coherent.** Moving to DeepSeek, I reordered every prompt so the fixed rules come first and the per-turn data last. Cache hits rose to about 80–89% and input cost fell sharply. Reordering is not free, though: rules must still read as one coherent instruction, and data must not be referenced before it appears. Cache-friendly layout is a design constraint to plan for from the start, not a late optimisation.

**1.4 Models are bad at prohibition.** Telling a model "never do X", especially with an example of X, puts X at the centre of its attention, and it sometimes does exactly that. Describing the desired behaviour and the principle behind it works better than listing forbidden outputs.

**1.5 Write prompts as principles, not case-by-case rules.** Each failing case tempts you to add a rule or an example copied from it. That fixes the case and breaks a neighbour, and the prompt grows into a patchwork. When the formulator mixed two needs in one search ("replace my ugly card and change my address"), I added the principle "search for the need itself, in general, non-personal terms", not a rule about ugly cards. Principles generalise; patches accumulate.

**1.6 [added for review] Plain, short wording beats elaborate wording.** Long qualified sentences ("carries no details of the request… limited to the listed fields of the logged-in customer") confused the model more than they helped. "An app screen is only a redirect: the transaction takes place on the bank app's own page" did the job.

## 2. Working with models

**2.1 Model variation is inevitable, but manageable by how work is split across prompts.** The same question can get different outcomes run to run (exact repeats agreed 16/20 in September). Prompt tuning cannot remove this. What reduced it was architecture: giving each judgment to a focused call, deciding deterministically wherever possible (routing, fallbacks, number substitution), and caching consensus answers. Concentrating work in one prompt multiplies variance; distributing it contains it.

**2.2 Placeholders should carry meaning.** Personal data and exact numbers are replaced by placeholders before any model call, so the model never sees real values. Opaque tokens made the model treat them as missing or broken. Tokens and field names whose words describe what they stand for, such as calling clarification options `clarification_guesses` rather than `candidate_replies`, let the model reason about them correctly. Models work on semantics, so a name is part of the prompt.

**2.3 [added for review] A stronger model is not a substitute for the right information.** Several wrong decisions (the sufficiency auditor calling an address "unavailable" right after it was fetched; the compliance screen imagining the bot executing transfers) came from the model not knowing something: the other tools' results, the bot's real service scope. Supplying the missing context fixed them; changing the model would not have.

**2.4 [added for review] Switching models is not a fix until it is measured on the hard cases.** Earlier runs suggested Gemini 3.1 blocked no ordinary questions while 3.5 did. On a replay that included the hard cases (customers reporting fraud or theft), 3.1 blocked the same 5 of 42 as 3.5. The earlier evidence came from question sets that happened not to contain those cases. A comparison is only as good as the cases it includes.

**2.5 Schemas make the messages between pipeline steps safe, but a schema also steers what the model writes, so it is not neutral for every task.** The pipeline passes JSON from one model call to the next. Where a call asked for JSON without a schema, Gemini occasionally returned something unreadable: a missing comma, an unquoted key, a stray "- " before a key, or prose. A deterministic scan of 8,178 replies across five runs found 19 malformed replies among 2,104 schema-less calls (0.9%) and none among 5,122 schema-constrained calls. The damage never showed as "bad JSON". It showed as other problems: an empty answer (BUG-065), or a fact-check verdict replaced by an "audit unavailable" placeholder, which wasted a revision round and ended in a generic fallback that discarded a correct answer. The cause was mundane: the schema was chosen by matching a phrase in the prompt, and a renamed prompt title silently stopped matching. Repairing broken JSON deterministically was not a real option, because the failures came in six different shapes and only two could be fixed without guessing. Giving the fact-checker and the two reply writers explicit schemas fixed it: 53 of 53 replayed prompts parsed, including all 9 that had failed, with no loss of content or audit accuracy (R5 still caught 30/30 planted errors).

The limitation appeared in the reviser, the one step that edits existing text instead of creating or judging it. With a schema, it kept a sentence the fact-checker had told it to remove in 7 of 10 repeated runs, against 0 of 5 without a schema. It usually added the requested replacement and left the flagged sentence in front of it. **Root cause: two mechanisms, one backed by research and one our own inference.**

1. *Committing before deciding (supported by research).* A model writes left to right and cannot go back and delete what it has already written. Under a strict schema the field order fixes the writing order, and the decoder checks only that each token is valid JSON, not that the content is right. So the model starts writing a block's text before it has worked out what must go. Once the flagged sentence is on the page, the best it can do is add the correction after it. Without a schema the model can plan the whole block first. Research agrees: format restrictions reduce reasoning quality while helping classification, and key order matters (Tam et al., 2024); constrained decoding distorts what the model would otherwise write (Park et al., 2024). Full references are in [LITERATURE_REVIEW.md](LITERATURE_REVIEW.md).
2. *A pull towards copying (our inference, no paper found).* The reviser is shown each block's current text and returns the same structure. A field with no description gives the model only its name and the value it was shown, which favours "copy, then change". The prompt's instruction to remove the flagged phrase sits far away in a long prompt.

Evidence, all on one case where the reviser should remove one flagged sentence (lower is better):

| Variant | Kept the flagged sentence |
|---|---|
| Plain schema | 7 of 10 |
| Text field moved after the other fields | 4 of 5 |
| A "phrases to remove" field written before the text (decide first) | 2 of 5 |
| Removal instruction written into the text field's description | 1 of 5 |
| No schema | 0 of 5 |

Making the model decide first helped (2 of 5), which supports mechanism 1. In 3 of the 4 runs where it listed the phrase first, it then removed it. But in one run it listed the sentence and still kept it, which mechanism 1 cannot explain, so something like mechanism 2 also plays a part. No variant matched the result without a schema. The reviser had produced no malformed JSON in 97 calls, so it was left without a schema.

The lessons: give every model-to-model message a schema chosen explicitly at the call site, never by matching prompt text; and treat schema field descriptions as instructions. A schema suits steps that judge, classify or create content in one pass. For a step that has to reason about what to delete before it writes, a schema can make the model commit too early, so measure the step's real job (here, removing flagged text) with and without the schema before adopting it.

**2.6 Reasoning before result was applied to the steps that judge, but not to the step that writes, and a writer can make a judgement too.** Every judging step (evidence check, compliance screen, fact-check, triage) writes its reasoning before its verdict. The reply writer was treated as a pure writing job and returns only the reply. One case showed the gap. A customer asked why their top-up had not been processed. The evidence check approved a general FAQ entry, which says Horizon holds *certain* transactions for 24 hours and is written for a customer who has been notified of a delay. It then told the writer to "admit that the top-up transaction is experiencing a temporary security hold". The writer is told to follow such notes, so it opened with "Your top-up transaction is currently experiencing a temporary security hold". The fact-checker never sees the note, and read the sentence as a reasonable conclusion. A FAQ is general information, not a check of this customer's account, and no step was asked to keep that distinction. This was rare: one note in 656 and one reply in about 9,000 made such a claim. So it was logged as a limitation (BUG-072) instead of changing the writer prompt, which shapes every reply, so late in the project. The design lesson: decide for each step, including the ones that write, whether it makes a judgement, and give that judgement its own reasoning field. Here the field would ask what the evidence establishes about this customer and what it does not. Also tell every step plainly what the evidence is: general guidance, not a finding about the customer.

**2.7 When code checks a label the model wrote, normalise the label first; never rely on the model copying a spelling exactly.** The query step tags each search with a product and an intent from a fixed table, and code then checks that the pair exists in the table. The model's output schema describes these fields ("names from the matrix") but allows any text. Product names use underscores (`Horizon_APP`, `Deposit_Account`) and intent names use spaces (`General Inquiries`), and the model sometimes carried the underscore style over: it wrote `General_Inquiries`. The check compared the text exactly, rejected a valid pair, and threw the search away, so the customer got "I do not have information" although an FAQ answered the question (BUG-073). Nothing was logged as an error; the case looked like a retrieval miss. A replay of all 1,610 logged searches found 8 discarded this way (0.5%), and 3 more where the misspelt intent was silently dropped from the filter. The fix was deterministic: match tags ignoring case, spaces, underscores and hyphens, then rewrite them in the table's own spelling, wherever tags meet the table. No prompt changed, an unknown tag is still rejected, and the replay showed that no valid search changed except to keep the intent the model meant. Two lessons. First, wherever code checks a label a model wrote, normalise it before the exact match. A fixed list of allowed values in the schema (an enum) would also have prevented this, which was not done here because it would have changed every query-step call this late in the project. Second, use one naming style across a vocabulary that a model has to reproduce. Mixing underscores and spaces invited the error.

## 3. Retrieval (RAG)

**3.1 Cosine similarity means similar in form, not the same in meaning.** A query mixing two needs, or wrapped in personal context, retrieves documents that resemble its wording rather than answer its need. Retrieval quality depends on what you search for as much as on how.

**3.2 Reciprocal Rank Fusion helps in selecting candidates.** Fusing the rankings of several query variants surfaced the right FAQ more reliably than any single ranking.

**3.3 Direct search is usually the best approach.** Searching with the customer's need stated directly gave less drift and less loss of meaning than rewritten queries. The formulator now writes a dedicated `search_query`, the need itself without reasons or personal details: "like telling a librarian what you want, not why".

**3.4 Three-angle matching of answer representations sometimes helps, at a higher cost.** Rephrasing the query from several angles occasionally retrieved an answer the direct query missed. It costs extra calls and can drift, so it is now a fallback, not the default.

**3.5 [added for review] Separate data problems from bot problems before fixing anything.** Of the BANKING77 questions the bot could not fully answer, 86% had no answering FAQ at all. No change to the bot can fix that, and measuring the bot on those questions only distorts its score. I now report performance on questions the FAQ covers and treat coverage as a content backlog. Some misses that looked like retrieval failures were FAQ tagging problems (a physical-card FAQ tagged only as credit card); the fix there was the data, not the code.

## 4. Auditing and compliance

**4.1 Inbound and outbound audits have different jobs.** The outbound audit checks a reply that exists. The inbound check must *predict* whether fulfilling a request would be non-compliant, before any reply is written. That is much harder than I expected.

**4.2 The regulated party is the bank, not the customer.** A customer cannot be non-compliant; MAS regulates Horizon. Framing the inbound check as "is this customer suspicious?" refused people asking for protection. Framing it as "if Horizon fulfilled this, would Horizon's response breach the rules?" answered them. That reframing was the key to the redesign.

**4.3 The outbound audit works better as one prompt.** Splitting groundedness and conduct checks across separate calls caused replies to bounce: one check's revision broke the other's. A combined audit, with all findings revised at once, converged faster and fell back less.

**4.4 Generation guidance is a good design for both auditing and generation.** The sufficiency auditor writes a short `generation_guidance` with each verdict, for example "admit there is no information on X, then mention Y". The generator follows it and the audit checks against it. That gives each stage a shared, explicit contract instead of each re-deriving what the reply should say.

## 5. Engineering practice and project management [added for review]

**5.1 Test sets must include the ordinary cases, not just the hard ones.** My compliance-screen tests used harmful prompts and protective questions but almost no plain balance or transfer requests, so the version that blocked core banking flows passed my tests. Every test set now includes a core-flow control group.

**5.2 Measure what you will report, and say how you measured it.** The first containment figure (22.5%) mixed questions the FAQ cannot answer with ones it can. Reporting on the FAQ-covered subset (66–70%), with the raw number beside it, is both fairer and more honest.

**5.3 Logging is what made debugging possible.** Every turn logs its full decision chain. Almost every root cause in this project (63 tracked issues) was found by reading those logs rather than by guessing. Log retention, by contrast, was too short (about 60 MB of rolling logs), so the test harness had to save its own copies.

**5.4 Fix root causes, and decide scope deliberately.** Each defect was traced to its cause before any fix was chosen, and several were consciously put out of scope (self-harm handling, model-failure handling, multi-account support) to protect the delivery. Saying "not in this MVP" clearly is as important as fixing.

**5.5 Free-tier infrastructure shapes the project.** Gemini's per-minute and daily limits stalled test runs, and running two batches in parallel only made it worse. I added a second provider (DeepSeek), key rotation and a clear "model unavailable" message. Operational limits belong in the plan from day one. Running replays in parallel later cost me again: two replays I thought I had stopped kept running, shared the same quota, and exhausted every key, so a day's comparison had to be redone. Background jobs need a reliable stop and one shared budget.

**5.6 [added for review] A defect found on the holdout must be fixed without tuning to the holdout.** The compliance over-blocking surfaced in the v5.1 holdout run, which my development tests had missed because they lacked fraud-report wording (lesson 5.1 again). I paused the evaluation, validated the fix mainly on development sets and the red team, and re-ran the holdout from the start on the fixed code, so the reported numbers come from the fixed system. The report says plainly where the defect was found.

## 6. What I would do differently

- Write user stories and a measurement plan in week one, and test against them continuously, rather than designing the metrics framework near the end.
- Give each prompt one job from the start, instead of discovering the limit through failures.
- Build a small regression set of core flows early and run it before every change.
- Plan for model and provider limits (quota, latency, cost) as part of the architecture.
- Give every check an explicit work scope and decide which component owns each risk before writing its prompt.
- Include boundary cases (benign requests that resemble harmful ones) in every safety test set from the start.
- Decide for every model step, writers included, whether it makes a judgement, and give each judgement a reasoning field before its output.
- Normalise every model-written label before matching it in code, constrain it to an enum where possible, and use one naming style for any vocabulary a model must reproduce.
- Give every model-to-model message an explicit schema from the start, and check after each change, by a deterministic scan of real replies, that every message still parses.
